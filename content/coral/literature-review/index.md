title: Thank Who for the Music - How Modes Of Music Access Shape Our Identities, Relationships And Cultures

## Introduction

For most of history we experienced exclusively *live* music in amphitheatres and domestic settings alike. With the introduction of recording and distribution, music shifted from an ephemeral experience into something you could own.

Physical media (vinyl records, cassettes, CDs) intertwined *listening* with *collection*. Shelves in bedrooms and living rooms both stored our collections and made our music identities visible.

Streaming services expanded our once discrete collections to the point of incomprehensible abundance. Now we have assumed access to essentially 'all' music, and if we don't mind ads, we don't even have to pay for it.

The only barrier between knowing a work of music exists and adding it to our collection is a 'save to library' button. 

In these platforms of democratised access, every song we repeat, skip, and interact with creates a clearer picture of our listening habits to better inform what gets shown to us, and to other people.

'Discovery' playlists drop us directly into our next favourite song or album without the labour of having to go look for it. This miracle of data science grants us deep familiarity with the music itself, without understanding or internalising any of its broader cultural or social context.

This is where the record shelf still holds its own against its contemporary replacement:  as a tangible interface between the collector, the collection, its aesthetics and culture, and the social community which gathers around it.

*Throughout, intertextual sources are read alongside the literature as primary resources.*

## Ourselves and Music

![[output-1.webp]]
*Campbell, C. (2023). [Cafe in Seoul]. Personal collection.*

Through the record shelf, the collector and the collection are in a state of constant dialogue. New music brings us back to our entire collection every time, re-acquainting us with what we already own in order to understand where it might best sit.

The longer we live with our collection, the stronger our relationship grows. We start to internalise meaning without ever having to commit to memory the exact location of our favourite album, or the one our friend lent us. This is what Polanyi calls 'tacit knowledge' - acquired "not by looking at things but by dwelling in them" (Polanyi, 1966, p.18). 

And by dwelling in our collections we also deepen our understanding of their interrelations. Polanyi suggests that our relationship towards individual albums (or songs) shifts from seeing them as discrete musical works to understanding how they might fit contextually into the rest of our music collection. DJs put this into practice when they 'select' a song during a set.

![[af6b7006-3cd1-4413-b8ba-6eb0461c4f1a-1-102-o.jpeg]]
*Sasaki, Rina. (2026). [Cameron DJing at Shady Lady, Fitzroy]. Courtesy of the Artist.*

Masterful track selection demands what Bourdieu (1984) calls 'cultural competence'. The crowd, the music and the DJ are in conversation with one another. As the room slips into different grooves, the DJ watches the ebb and flow of the crowd's energy and responds with the next record. The DJ has intimate knowledge of their music collection, and through this knowledge they are able to shape this unfolding conversation - "the capacity to see (*voir*) is a function of the knowledge (*savoir*)" (Bourdieu, 1984, p.2).

In their participation, the crowd is also implicitly acquiring cultural competence by simply being in contact with it - "This mastery is, for the most part, acquired simply by contact with works of art" (Bourdieu, 1984, p.4).

Spotify's 'DJ' by comparison has a computationally sophisticated understanding of our listening histories (*savoir*) but it has no capacity to see (*voir*). Its algorithm doesn't dwell in our libraries; it collects data. Its knowledge is incomplete, so it is blind. When we open our streaming apps we don't see our music identity reflected back at us, but a computer's blind interpretation of it.

![[screenshot-2026-06-18-at-1-00-50-pm.png]]
*Campbell, C. (2026). [Screenshot of Spotify Home]. Personal collection.*

What we like (playlists we made, albums we saved) and what we might like (recommended stations, discover weekly, daily mix) are shown side by side and with no weighting. Our all-time favourite album is no more spatially important than something we haven't even listened to. Not only do streaming services fail to 'see' our music identity, but they make seeing it for ourselves harder.

When our collections were physical, the only interpreters of our listening habits were other people, and unlike the algorithm, they can actually see.

"We assume that we can get to know someone through their tastes...eyeing someone's book and record shelves the first time we visit them" (Frith, 1996, p.5)

Tastes feel like a window through which you can understand somebody, but as you look through that window your own reflection is always staring back at you.

When you observe others' collections, you're judging their cultural competences against your own. Frith calls these cultural judgements self-revealing. You expose your identity not only by asserting what you like but also by what you can't stand. Being a hater is as important to socialising your cultural identity as being a lover.

The point of all this judgement isn't to agree on everything, but to understand "that conversation, argument, is possible." (Frith, 1996, p.5). We relate to culture and each other by exploring our identities together.

If we are faced with the challenge of *defending* our identities, our discussions start to resemble a turn based battle. Each person must prove they match the cultural competence of their opponent, then raise the stakes by offering increasingly niche references.

![[img-1890.png]]

The online music rating platform RateYourMusic has a reputation for a kind of musical elitism that is also present in these 'niche-offs'. RYM hosts millions of written reviews, and each of them must be assigned a corresponding number score. This isn't a natural human instinct for art critique  - ask most people for their thoughts on an album and it's unlikely they will respond with "2 out of 5" unless they're a RYM user.

![[screenshot-2026-06-18-at-1-15-15-pm.png]]
*Campbell, C. (2026). [Screenshot of RateYourMusic ]. Personal collection.*

Our emotional experiences with music are complex and unorganised. The score system is statistically clean. It's an algorithm imposing its way of thinking onto our experiences, flattening them into data it can extract and average to two decimal places.

By reducing value to a figure, the score itself is depleted of the very value it claims to represent.

Critique isn't inherently de-valuing however; it helps us communicate our likes, dislikes, and *why* something resonates with us, but over-analysis puts us at risk of getting lost in the details. "We must learn to *see* more, to *hear* more, to *feel* more... The aim of all commentary on art now should be to make works of art...more, rather than less, real to us" (Sontag, 1964, p.10).

Music is for *listening* after all, and sharing it with each other brings us together.

## Community and Music

Let's return to the club where our crowd is implicitly acquiring cultural competence through their contact with a masterful DJ at work.

If you asked most of the people in that crowd why they were there, they wouldn't say "to build my cultural competence through contact with it", they would probably say something like "because I love the way this DJ makes me feel" or "why are you talking to me on the dance floor, I'm trying to dance". They're not just *listening* to music - they are socially and emotionally entangled with it.

This distinction is something Frith describes as being about "ways of listening, about ways of hearing, about ways of being." (Frith, 1996, p.8) Our music tastes permeate through to our attitudes and sensibilities more broadly.

Punks have Mohawks, wear Doc Martens and Vivienne Westwood and endorse the political messaging of anarchism. Charli XCX's Brat fans share a love of party culture, being a little messy, and the aesthetic essentials of "a pack of cigs, a Bic lighter, and a strappy white top with no bra." (Charli XCX, 2024).

These groups express their identities by gathering their looks, language, and attitudes around the artists they share an appreciation for. If you are a punk rocker, surely you *must* wear Doc Martens, Vivienne Westwood and a Mohawk.

As people use music like this it has "come to have social ‘valency’ in and through their circumstances of use." (DeNora, 2000, p.6). Punk music didn't *make* people dress and act like that, but the association between the two developed through the ways punk rockers *used* punk music.

Considering how social media platforms can facilitate the development and expression of shared music identities like this on the internet inevitably leads us to look at MySpace.

![[old-myspace-website-music-player-via-yahoo.png]]
*Pei Pei [@peixz]. (2010). [Screenshot of MySpace music artist profile] [Screenshot]. Retrieved from Consequence. https://consequence.net/2019/03/myspace-lost-music-files-no-backup/*

MySpace allowed users to aesthetically personalise their profiles with HTML and CSS, upload and play music from their profiles and connect directly with the bands they loved and the fanbases who supported them.

Shklovski shows how MySpace functioned as a music identity broadcast "fans use music to express who they are. As part of this self-presentation, fans often select their favorite, sometimes obscure songs to stream from their profiles." (Shklovski & boyd, 2006, p.3)

We've already seen how communities form around shared music identities, and MySpace supported this by providing an online shelf for them. The habit of displaying 'obscure' songs helped us connect to people with shared tastes while avoiding the trap of musical elitism by not insinuating hierarchical value with a quantitative score.

MySpace was a music platform that let the music do the social gathering without forcing the interpretation of it. Streaming services made finding music easier, but the digital shelf our identities lived on was left behind, and replaced with summaries of our listening habits we can share to our stories.

## Music's Digital Architecture

The Spotify-Wrapped-ification of our listening habits don't feel like our music identities because they were generated *for* us, not *by* us. The physical and digital shelves of the past were something we put together with our own hands and HTML.

Streaming platforms operate with systemised efficiency. They build walled gardens for us - all the music we could ever want at our fingertips, and if the overabundance of choice gives us decision paralysis - discover weekly is only a tap away.

Manovich describes how the interests of databases are at odds with our identity expressions: "the database and narrative are natural enemies" (Manovich, 2001, p.225). Our music identities and the shelves we use to display them are narratives built on dwelling and careful arrangement. Our streaming platforms are databases built with searchable indexes and infinite catalogues. Spotify Wrapped and the like are attempts to reconcile this natural opposition by building something that looks like a narrative from the data it has collected on us.

![[00b42508-a20b-4746-8de5-234e1f2420b9-1-105-c.jpeg]]
*Campbell, C. (2024). [Screenshot of Spotify Wrapped]. Personal collection.*

This constructed narrative provides an insight into our listening tastes, but the 'nobody wants to see your Spotify wrapped' discourse resurfaces at the end of every year. This begs the question: does nobody want to see your music tastes, or does nobody care about your platform's interpretations of it?

Van Dijck provides a framework to unpack the social underpinnings of this distaste. Online ecosystems have shifted from prioritising *connectedness* to *connectivity*. MySpace allowed for *connectedness* by giving us shelves and online meeting spaces, Spotify's interests are in *connectivity*: recommendations, Spotify music in your Instagram stories, engagement statistics. It feels customised for you without requiring any input.

"Customization seems to have become less the art of soliciting customers’ needs than the science of engineering their desires." (van Dijck, 2013, p.170)

Streaming services aren't a representation of our music tastes, they are a simulation of them.

If we want to build the narrative of our music tastes, we have to look for a platform that allows for more user construction. Music influencers, with no other platform left to congregate to move Instagram, fighting for attention amongst cat videos, sponsored brand deals, and the rest of our doom scrolling content.

![[screenshot-2026-06-18-at-1-28-39-pm.png]]
*Campbell, C. (2026). [Screenshot of @marg.mp3 Profile]. Personal collection.*

Taste makers like Margeaux Labat @marg.mp3 have successfully built followings around sharing their music identities with their audiences, but platforms like instagram aren't egalitarian "popularity rankings filter out people who are less "valuable" than others." (van Dijck, 2013, p.159).

The walled gardens of our streaming platforms have forced people to look elsewhere in an attempt to express their music identities, but these platforms have their own associated problems, and in the trade-off we lose rich interactivity with the music we discover - we can't save Margeux's recommendation to our library with the tap of a button. 

The database and the expression of our cultural identities don't have to be mutually exclusive, we've already seen that with platforms like Letterboxd.

![[cameron-campbell.png]]
*Campbell, C. (2026). [Screenshot of Letterboxd Profile]. Personal collection.*

Manovich calls Letterboxd a 'hypernarrative', a number of database records linked together so that more than one trajectory is possible. "merely to create these trajectories is of course not sufficient; the author also has to control the semantics of the elements and the logic of their connection so that the resulting object will meet the criteria of a narrative" (Manovich, 2001, p.228). Letterboxd allows users to navigate their database through curious exploration, add movies to their 'top 4', create personal lists, leave comments and reviews, and follow other movie enthusiasts.

Letterboxd's hypernarrative structure hosts both an abundant catalogue to explore and a shelf for us to collect, dwell in, and socialise our tastes. It has an active community (over 96 million reviews) which can lean towards a RateYourMusic style audience with number scoring, but also supports the more qualitative viewer by allowing reviews to be submitted without a figure fixed to them. The precedent for an online gathering space for cultural debate and engagement is clear, and in it an opportunity presents itself for the online revival of the *music* shelf.

## Conclusion

Our music collections and our identities inform one another. By dwelling in our collections we form deeper connections with the individual works as well as the sum of their parts.

Dwelling was built into our physical libraries through the record shelf, if you had a collection - you were dwelling in it. The shelf also communicated your tastes simply by existing. Because the tastes in the shelf are legible, it allowed us the opportunity to engage in debate, and provided opportunities for building relationships around them.

Streaming democratised our access to 'all' music but separated our ability to dwell in it by design. Our platforms of access have intimate knowledge of our listening histories, so the music it recommends us is almost statistically certain to resonate with us. But in streamlining discovery we lost a personal narrative that used to come with it. These discoveries can't be traced because they were handed to us. 

Where the record shelf is socialised identity, streaming is individualised abundance.

The communities we form around music help us connect with the embodied *experiences* of it. From gathering together to watch a DJ and gain new cultural competences, to influencing how we communicate our language, styles and attitudes - music is as much about ways of *being* as it is ways of *knowing* and *hearing*. 

Some online communities encourage critique, which helps us understand the reasons why certain music might resonate with us, but risks over-analysis, and by extension, depletes our *experience* of it. Other communities simple encouraged the presentation of tastes without critique, and helped us connect with the musicians and fanbases we aligned with. 

Our streaming gardens of musical abundance are walled off from one another, and by extension, we are walled off from the communities which exist inside them. These walled gardens are a design choice, and we are free to explore alternatives. 

We can build a new garden. One that keeps the abundant space to explore and express ourselves - but without the walls. A garden of biodiversity which can be tended to by anybody who wishes to visit it.
