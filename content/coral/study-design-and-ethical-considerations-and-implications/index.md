## Abstract
For most of human history we listened to exclusively *live* music. With the introduction of recording and distribution, our experiences with music shifted from an ephemeral experience into something we could *own* and *collect*. Shelves in our bedrooms, living spaces and listening rooms allowed us to archive and dwell in our collections, broadcast our music identities, and engage in cultural discussions and debates with the people who saw them.

The contemporary music ecosystem has fractured the shelf. Our collections live in streaming services amongst the discovery algorithms and the entirety of their catalogues, our music identities are diluted by our democratised access to them. Our ability to socialise our tastes and host discussion has been relegated to niche online communities which mostly value quantitive evaluations (assigning a numerical score).

Reading nine sources across, philosophy, sociology, and media theory with integrated 'on the ground' referencing to popular culture and screenshot examples of digital music ecosystems, this review establishes what the record shelf did for our relationship with music, how the architecture of its digital replacements fractured it, and what opportunities might exist to recover the parts which were left behind.

## Research Question
How do people construct and inhabit their personal music ecosystems across physical and streaming media, and how do they develop inter-relational understandings about music and the people they share it with in these ecosystems.

## Methodological Framing
For semester one of my Honours my overarching methodology has been research *through* Design (rather than *into* design or *for* design).

Knowledge about my field is produced through the act of designing and building a prototype. The primary metric for guiding this is intuitive, i.e. *does this feel right?*

This intuition is grounded in practise led auto-ethnography. I am a musician, DJ, and avid listener and community participant. I spend a lot of time organising my music library, I quit Spotify last year and tried 9 different music streaming services which I had to migrate my library between each time, and through that experience it became clear to me how socially closed off our music identities become when we don't share the same streaming platforms.

While this approach has informed my research in semester 1, moving into semester 2 I want to engage with the community. 

This will take the form of surveys, interviews and user testing.
### Surveys
This is the widest capture of information to better understand the relationships between people and music. Questions will focus on the following:
#### Ecosystem and Hardware
What platforms people use, if they have migrated, how they listen (headphones, speakers), how often they take on the role of a 'music selector' in social environments.
#### Organisation
How do people keep their music libraries, regularly updating playlists with coherent vibes (chill afternoon, dinner party, etc.), chronological (May 2026, June 2026, etc.), if they keep physical collections as well.
#### Discovery
Ranking how they discover music from most to least engaged with. Recommendations from friends, discover algorithms, the radio, youtube, live shows, festivals, etc.

#### Identity and practice signals
How people share and develop their music tastes with others: showing screenshots of Spotify wrapped, scrolling playlists together, posting playlists to stories, following music influencers, etc.

The data collected in this will by default be anonymous, with the ability to include contact information for follow up interviews where the questions expand on the themes in the survey.
### Interviews
The interviews provide qualitative insight into the broad picture captured from the survey.

The style will be conversational with the above themes acting as points to return to. (Ecosystem and Hardware, Organisation, Discovery, Identity and Practise Signals).

For example: the interview questions will not be "What genre of music do you like" or "Would you use a feature like x", but more of a conversational exploration of their frustrations, affiliations, and socialisations towards their experiences of music, both in and outside of their streaming service or social media platforms. 

This interview style is present throughout DeNora's book Music in Everyday Life. It provides a framework for people to express their lived experiences first before interpreting them.

The participant selection for interviews would prioritise variation across the spectrum of music professionals, music enthusiasts, and casual listeners.
#### Format
1-on-1. Audio recorded with consent, transcribed, aiming for length of 45-60min.
### User Testing
To evaluate the built prototype against real world use cases. The prototype is built from my own intuition, so this needs to happen in order to see if the relational / spatial hierarchy is legible and navigable. This is to determine function *not* aesthetics.

This would be ideally after the closed public beta is live, but failing that it will be conducted with an interactive prototype on my own laptop.

To gain meaningful insight, the users will be given a task: e.g. Find out who your favourite artist has collaborated with, see if you can find connections between your favourite artists.
### Study Plan and Sequence
The aim of the participant based research in this document is to move from breadth to deep evaluation.

Surveys feed the interview shortlist, interviews and auto-ethnography feed the prototype, and the prototype feeds the user testing.
#### Sequence
1. **Mar-Jun 2026** Sem 1 (complete) - Practise led autoethnography
   *no external participants, no ethics clearance required*
2. **Jul 2026** Early Sem 2 - Ethics application submitted and approved before participant contact
3. **Post Approval** Early Sem 2 - Survey distributed → breadth data + interview shortlist
4. **Aug-Sep 2026** Mid Sem 2 - Interviews → depth data
5. **Sep-Oct** Late Sem 2 - User testing on live build (or prototype fallback)
### Ethics and Risk
Because I will be engaging with participant based research I need to ensure my ethics application is submitted and approved before moving forward. 

The survey will be anonymous, but it will still need to communicate what the information being gathered is about, what they will do, the fact that it is voluntary, and who is handling the data, and how to get in touch with them.

This ability to self select for interview follow up introduces the need for participants to indicate their understanding and consent, which will also be required for the interviews and the user testing. 
### Ethical Questions
More broadly speaking this project has ethical questions relating to where it sits amongst the ecosystem it criticises. I have tried to keep my research and writing as balanced as possible "streaming democratised access which is a good thing but it made socialising harder which is a bad thing". 

Even if Coral is an attempt to bring more meaning and social context to digital music identities, it still has to extract and interpret user information like the platforms which received critique for doing so in the literature review. 

This tension between critique and opportunity keeps the goals of Coral clear. Building a music platform which gives the users ability to feel more in control of their discovery and presentation without requiring completely manual input by incorporating *some* of the algorithmic processing present in alternative environments.
